Lesson 6 of 10

The Quantum System: Defining Edit Scope

Reads ~20 min · Hands-on: predict the boundaries of four quantums at one cursor

A Quantum is a stateless boundary strategy: given a cursor position, a span flag, and the document's newline indices, it returns two integers marking the region to move through, highlight, or delete. Everything else in Visur's quantum system is bookkeeping around those two integers.

Mission Anchor

Lesson 5 kept tripping over one word: quantum. It appeared as a handler family (ChangeCursorQuantumHandler), as two submodes (quantumStart, quantumEnd), as a scope value (scopeQuantum), and as a status-bar readout. Every one of those is a different door into the same room. This lesson opens the room.

The itinerary says there are "4 quantum types" and that "RegexQuantum—the search forwards/backwards operators use RegexQuantum to define search regions." The first half is close; the second half is wrong, and correcting it teaches you how the pieces actually fit together. Along the way we find a dead parameter that survives in a method signature used thirteen times.

Goal this lesson: Given a key sequence, name the quantum it selects, say whether that quantum is the cursor or scope quantum, and predict the two integer bounds it computes. Name the one parameter of getBoundaries that no caller ever reads.

1. A Quantum Is Not a Region

The instinct is to imagine a Quantum as an object holding a selection—a start and an end. It is not. The whole abstraction is three methods, and none of them stores anything:

The region itself lives in the editor model, not in the Quantum. Three fields carry it, and each answers a different question:

Model valueTypeQuestion it answers
cursorQuantum Quantum (a global var) Which strategy is currently active?
cursorQuantumStart / cursorQuantumEnd two ints What region did the strategy last compute?
span int (a global var, 0 or 1) Is that region active as a selection?

Getters at EditorModelCoupler.java:156–174, setters at 295–335.

Why this design pays off. Because the Quantum has no state, one singleton instance per type is enough—they are registered once at startup and shared forever. LineQuantum is a single object; "the current line" is re-derived from ca and nl on every call. There is no per-selection allocation and no stale bounds to invalidate.
Impure in practice. The contract looks pure, but getBoundaries reads ServiceHolder.editorModelCoupler and several implementations write back: LineQuantum reads emc.getCY(), RegexQuantum calls emc.putCA(...), and DocumentQuantum can reset the span. "Compute a region" has side effects. Treat every getBoundaries call as a potential state change.

2. The Four Registered Quantums

initializeQuantums() registers four instances, then wires the keys. Reading the registration line by line is the fastest way to see what "quantum type" really means here:

NameClassKeyRegion it computes
character CharacterQuantum q one character: {ca, ca+1}
word RegexQuantum("word", "\\S+") w the run of matching characters around ca
line LineQuantum e the current long line, from nl
document DocumentQuantum r the whole document: {0, content.length()}
Correction 1 — the name is word, not regex. The itinerary lists RegexQuantum as one of the four quantum types. It is a quantum class, and the instance is registered under the name "word". This matters because RegexQuantum is parameterized—(name, regexSource) (RegexQuantum.java:9–13)—so a second regex quantum (say "identifier") could be added without a single new Java class. The "type" is the registry entry, not the class.
Correction 2 — search does not use RegexQuantum. The itinerary claims the f/F search operators use RegexQuantum to define search regions. They do not. SearchForwardOp.java:18–22 calls emc.getScopeQuantum().getBoundaries(...) and then a plain String.indexOf(searchTarget). The region searched is bounded by the scope quantum (a line by default), and the match itself is a literal substring, not a regex. RegexQuantum is used only for the word cursor quantum. We return to the scope quantum in §6.

Bounds are half-open: [start, end). The frontend proves it. Its span highlight is absX >= cursorQuantumStart && absX < cursorQuantumEnd (visur.js:145–157). So a character quantum over index 3 is {3, 4}, and a document quantum over a 100-character file is {0, 100}—not {99}. When you predict bounds, add one to the last covered index.
Correction 3 — LineQuantum.getBoundaries does not use RelativeLineBoundCalculator. The itinerary says it does. The boundary method inlines its own arithmetic: nl.get(cy) and nl.get(cy - 1) (LineQuantum.java:10–24). The calculator is used on the movement path (LineQuantum.java:87–102) and by CharacterQuantum's up/down movement. "Line boundaries" and "line movement" are computed by different code.

5. RelativeLineBoundCalculator and the nl Array

Both line boundary code and line movement depend on one global variable, nl, whose values are rebuilt every time content changes. It is shorter and stranger than its name suggests:

From that one array, the calculator derives two different notions of a line:

FunctionFormulaMeaning
getLongLineLength(cy, nl) nl[cy] - nl[cy-1] (or nl[0]) the logical line, including its newline
getShort(cx, cy, nl) [{k·width}, {next multiple of width}] the wrapped on-screen row containing cx

RelativeLineBoundCalculator.java:7–14 (long) and 16–48 (short). The short-line math is what lets the cursor walk up and down a wrapped line without skipping a visual row.

Long line vs short line is a wrapping concept. A paragraph that wraps over three screen rows is one long line and three short lines. e (line quantum) spans the long line; up/down movement moves by short line. That split is why the calculator has both getLongLineLength and getShort.

6. Cursor Quantum, Scope Quantum, Span

The word "quantum" names three related but distinct roles in the running editor. Keeping them apart is the core skill of this lesson:

7. [ and ]: Choosing Scope vs Cursor at Runtime

The quantumStart and quantumEnd submodes are where the cursor/scope distinction gets decided dynamically. QuantumStartSubmodeHandler resolves the key to a name and compiles a one-shot program:

Inside Quantum.quantumStart(), two quantums race. The handler's named quantum (from the key) and the current cursor quantum are compared by the size of the region they would cover at span 1; the larger one becomes the scope, the smaller becomes the cursor:

quantumStart() then moves ca to the scope start; quantumEnd() moves it to the scope end (minus one when a span is active). Both finish by calling setSmallestQuantum, which installs the smaller quantum as the cursor quantum if it is not already.

The comparison is what makes the gesture composable. Because the choice is computed from region size, [w and [r work without special cases: whichever of {current cursor quantum, keyed quantum} is bigger sets the destination, and the smaller one stays as your cursor. One rule, four quantum types, two directions.

9. Primary Source

The code remains the highest-trust account, and the quantum files are small enough to read end to end in one sitting. Read in this order:

  1. Quantum.java—the three abstract methods plus quantumStart()/quantumEnd(). This is the single most important file in the lesson.
  2. CharacterQuantum.java and LineQuantum.java—the two simplest boundary computations, side by side.
  3. RegexQuantum.java—the only one with real search logic; read getQuantumStart/getQuantumEnd first.
  4. RelativeLineBoundCalculator.java—long line vs short line, and why nl is enough to derive both.

For the underlying design idea—separating "what region am I acting on" from "how do I move within it"—the deepest well is Vim's own motion and text-object documentation: Vim's motion.txt and visual.txt. Vim's iw ("inner word"), aw ("a word"), and ip ("inner paragraph") are the same idea as RegexQuantum("word", "\\S+"), but keyed by operator+motion instead of held as a single cursor-quantum state. Comparing the two designs is the most valuable thing to take from this lesson.

For wisdom on where this abstraction breaks down, the Vi and Vim Stack Exchange has years of practitioners arguing about text-object edge cases (empty lines, trailing whitespace, multi-byte characters). Their answers are a living record of the boundary decisions your quantums also face.

10. Key Takeaways

  • A Quantum has no state. Three methods (getBoundaries, move, getName); the region lives in cursorQuantumStart/End and span.
  • Four registered quantums: character, word (a RegexQuantum), line, document—keyed q w e r.
  • Two lookup maps: KeyToQuantumName then QuantumNameToQuantum. One key table feeds changeCursorQuantum, changeScope, quantumStart, and quantumEnd.
  • Bounds are half-open [start, end); includeTail is a dead fourth parameter; RegexQuantum powers the word cursor quantum, not search.
  • Search is fenced by the scope quantum (default line, changed by /), not by any regex.
  • nl is "start of next line": line start is nl[cy-1], long-line length is nl[cy] - nl[cy-1], and the last element is content.length().
  • [/] choose scope vs cursor by region size, then move to that scope's start or end.
